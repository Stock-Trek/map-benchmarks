# Executive Summary: Rust Map Benchmark Results

**Prepared:** 2026-10-25

**Data:** 1,323 Criterion measurements, 112 benchmark groups, 19 map implementations (Criterion 0.8.2, rustc 1.99.0).

**Metric:** Criterion median batch time (`median.point_estimate`). No baseline or prior-run comparison was available.

## Bottom line

There is no single “best” Rust map. The right choice depends on whether the workload is single-threaded or concurrent, whether keys are trusted, whether iteration or ordering matters, and whether the workload needs cheap snapshots or heavy mutation.

Five practical conclusions stand out:

1. **For ordinary single-threaded workloads, the safe default is still `std::HashMap` (or `hashbrown` with a DoS-resistant hasher).** When every map uses the same hasher, `std`, `hashbrown`, and `ahash` are usually within a few percent of each other for insert, lookup, get-or-insert, and growth. The large out-of-the-box lead of `rustc-hash` is mostly its FxHash hasher, not a fundamentally faster table.
2. **`rustc-hash` is the fastest out-of-the-box option for trusted/internal keys.** It wins 26 of the 37 serial groups it enters (40.6% of all 64 serial groups). Do not use it for attacker-controlled keys: FxHash is not designed to resist hash-flooding.
3. **`indexmap` is the clear choice when iteration or insertion order matters.** It wins all six iteration groups, and is 4.1× faster than the next implementation at 100K entries. Its wrapper uses `swap_remove`, so order changes after removals unless the application uses a stable-removal API.
4. **`txmap` is the broad concurrent winner, but the result is not hasher-controlled.** It wins 34 of 48 concurrent groups (70.8%) and is within 10% in 42 of 48. However, concurrent benchmarks are out-of-the-box only, and `txmap` benefits from its default `rapidhash`; `scc`, `papaya`, and `dashmap` default to `RandomState`, while `starshard` uses `FxBuildHasher`. Treat the concurrent ranking as provisional until rerun with a common or production hasher.
5. **Specialists beat generalists in their niches.** `leapfrog` wins 11 of 24 concurrent u64 groups; `scc` wins some String write-heavy/write-only cases; `papaya` wins pure String read-only contention; `rpds-hash-trie-map` clones in 2.18 ns via structural sharing; `intmap` wins u64 lookups/removals; `horde` wins same-hasher removals. None of these specialists is a good universal default.

## What the data shows

### Serial workloads: hasher choice dominates the headline

`rustc-hash` is the most frequent serial winner, but it only appears in out-of-the-box groups because its benchmark wrapper cannot accept a custom hasher. With `CommonHasher = ahash::RandomState`, the picture changes:

- `std`, `hashbrown`, and `ahash` form a tight cluster for most point operations.
- `hashbrown` is the most consistent runner-up: 25 of 64 serial groups within 10% of the fastest, despite only two outright wins.
- `ahash` wins the same-hasher clear-and-reuse groups and is usually within a few percent elsewhere.
- `indexmap` wins same-hasher String insert/growth as well as every iteration group.
- `intmap` wins u64 lookup-hit, lookup-miss, and remove out-of-the-box, but is weak on insert, growth, and iteration.
- Ordered maps (`btreemap`, `crossbeam-skiplist`) win no serial groups, and persistent maps (`imbl`, `rpds-hash-trie-map`, `immutable-chunkmap`) win only construction or clone groups, not insert/lookup/remove/iterate.

The practical read: **choose `std`/`hashbrown` by default; choose `rustc-hash` only when keys are trusted and maximum single-thread speed matters; choose `indexmap` when scans or insertion order matter; choose `intmap` only for integer-keyed, lookup/removal-heavy work.**

### Clone cost is misleading unless the clone is mutated

`rpds-hash-trie-map` clones a 100K map in 2.18 ns; `rustc-hash` takes 56.6 µs and `std` 74.5 µs. That makes persistent maps look extraordinarily attractive for snapshotting.

But the advantage disappears when the clone is modified:

| 100K workload | `rustc-hash` | `ahash` | `rpds-hash-trie-map` | `immutable-chunkmap` |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Clone only | 56.6 µs | 63.1 µs | **2.18 ns** | 3.94 ns |
| Clone then write | **179.6 µs** | 188.5 µs | 3.51 ms | 5.18 ms |

Use persistent maps when snapshots are mostly read; benchmark the actual clone-then-mutate pattern before adopting them for write-heavy work.

### Concurrent workloads: one broad winner, several narrow ones

`txmap` is the only concurrent map that is broadly competitive: 34/48 group wins and 42/48 within 10%. It wins most contention and synchronization mixes and most 2-thread throughput groups.

The exceptions matter:

- **`leapfrog`** wins 11/24 u64 groups, including hot-spot Zipfian and read-heavy u64 synchronization. It requires `K: Copy` and `V: Copy`, so it does not apply to String keys.
- **`scc`** wins 2-thread String write-only synchronization and 3-thread String write-heavy throughput.
- **`papaya`** wins 3-thread String read-only synchronization; `txmap` is 18% slower in that case.
- **`dashmap`** is never fastest but is close in a few String write-heavy cases.
- **`crossbeam-skiplist`** and **`starshard`** never place within 10% of the fastest concurrent result.

The practical read: **start concurrent mixed workloads with `txmap`, but validate the hasher and the specific contention pattern. Use `leapfrog` for `Copy`-keyed u64 hot-spot workloads, `scc` for some String write-heavy cases, and `papaya` for read-only String contention.**

### Stability is good overall, but close concurrent calls need re-running

Most medians are stable: the median relative MAD is 0.53%, and 90% of measurements have relative MAD at or below 2.1%. Only 45 of 1,323 measurements (3.4%) meet the report’s high-variance screen (MAD ≥ 5% or SD ≥ 10%).

The noise is concentrated in:

- concurrent write-heavy/write-only synchronization (`dashmap`, `starshard`) and read-only synchronization for `crossbeam-skiplist`;
- persistent-map iteration/growth (`rpds-hash-trie-map`, `imbl`);
- a few fast concurrent runs with heavy-tailed samples (`leapfrog`, `txmap`, `scc`).

Several close calls are within measurement noise, including `dashmap`’s 2-thread String write-only result (18.9% MAD) and `scc`’s 3-thread String write-only result (5.1% MAD). Do not treat a 1–2% ordering as definitive without re-running.

## What this analysis does not tell us

- **No memory usage, allocation rate, or tail latency.** A map with a slightly worse median may still be preferable for memory footprint or predictable p99.
- **No repeated independent runs.** Stability is based on Criterion’s within-run dispersion, not run-to-run reproducibility.
- **No same-hasher concurrent comparison.** The concurrent ranking mixes map design with default hasher choice.
- **No async runtime, long-lived pinning, or reclamation-pause measurements.** `scc` is tested through its synchronous APIs; `horde`/`papaya` pinning costs are included per operation but not under realistic guard lifetimes.
- **No workload beyond 3 threads.** Scaling beyond three workers, NUMA effects, and oversubscription are unmeasured.
- **No range-query, serialization, or crash-recovery benchmarks.** Ordered maps are represented only by point operations and full iteration.

## Recommended next steps

1. **Pick candidates by workload shape, not by a single leaderboard.** Use `std`/`hashbrown` as the safe default; add `rustc-hash`, `indexmap`, `intmap`, `rpds`, or `txmap` only where the workload matches their strengths.
2. **Re-run the final concurrent candidate with the production hasher.** In particular, compare `txmap`, `leapfrog`, `scc`, and `papaya` under a common hasher before standardizing.
3. **Benchmark the real mutation pattern for persistent maps.** If the application clones and then writes, use clone-then-write; clone-only numbers are not representative.
4. **Re-run close or high-variance cases.** Focus on concurrent String write-heavy/write-only synchronization and any decision that depends on a 1–2% difference.
5. **Add memory and tail-latency measurements** before making a final production choice, especially for large maps and latency-sensitive services.
