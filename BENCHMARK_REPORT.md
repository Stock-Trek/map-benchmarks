### Reading the numbers

Criterion reports a **median** (`median.point_estimate`) and a **median absolute deviation** (`median_abs_dev.point_estimate`). The median is the middle of the sampled per-iteration times; compared with the mean, it is less affected by a few slow samples. MAD is the median of `|sample − median|`, a robust measure of typical spread. `relMAD = MAD / median` is used below to compare spread across benchmarks of different sizes. Criterion also reports standard deviation (`std_dev.point_estimate`); it is more sensitive to outliers, so the stability section uses both.

Tables show **median time per Criterion iteration**, not per individual map operation, unless the operation description says otherwise. For example, `create` is 10,000 constructions, and `growth` is n insertions. Setup work is excluded by `iter_batched` where the benchmark needs a pre-built map; concurrent worker spawn, pinning, and join are also outside the timed region.

A ratio such as `1.09×` means the contender took 1.09 times the fastest time, i.e. 9% slower. The close-contender cutoff is 1.10×. Tables omit implementations more than 10% behind the fastest; the full set remains in Criterion's output. Criterion's on-disk identifier for each timing is `target/criterion/<group>/<implementation>/new/estimates.json`; the group path supplies the operation, size, key type, hasher mode, and thread count, so the variant labels above map directly to the corresponding directory.

### Trade-offs

- **Hasher choice can dominate the out-of-the-box ranking.** `rustc-hash` uses FxHash, which is extremely fast for trusted keys and gives it large out-of-box wins. When every map uses ahash, `hashbrown`, `std`, and `ahash` converge to within a few percent on most point operations. The right conclusion is “choose FxHash when the key space is trusted,” not “the underlying table is 30% faster.”
- **Safety costs speed.** `std`'s randomized `RandomState` is the safer default against HashDoS; `rustc-hash` gives that up. `ahash` is keyed and fast, but it is not a cryptographic hash and should not be treated as equivalent to `RandomState` for adversarial inputs.
- **Iteration and point operations pull in opposite directions.** `indexmap`'s dense insertion-order layout explains its 2× iteration lead, while its point lookups/inserts/removes are slower. It also wins String growth under a common hasher, a separate advantage worth validating on your key distribution. `btreemap` trades point-operation speed for ordering and range capability.
- **Cheap clone is not cheap copy-on-write.** `rpds-hash-trie-map`, `imbl`, and `immutable-chunkmap` clone in nanoseconds, but clone-then-write is 15.8–32.1× slower than `rustc-hash`. Use them when snapshots are read-mostly, not when every clone is immediately mutated.
- **Concurrent design is workload-specific.** `txmap` is the best general choice, but `leapfrog` owns u64 read-heavy/skewed patterns and `papaya` owns String read-only. `leapfrog`'s lead comes with hard `Copy` restrictions, so it is not a drop-in replacement for a general map.
- **Key size changes the answer.** For `u64` and `String<16>`, several maps are within about 7% under the same hasher. For `String<128>`, only `ahash` and `hashbrown` are within 10% of the winner, and the absolute lookup cost is ~2.9× the `String<16>` cost. Long keys deserve their own benchmark.
- **Memory footprint and tail latency are not measured.** These results say nothing about bytes per entry, allocator behavior under fragmentation, or p99/p999 latency. The stability section covers run-to-run spread, not production tail latency.

| sync 2T, String<32>, write-only | `dashmap` | 817.486 µs | 74.304 µs | 9.1% | 10.6% | Lock-based write contention is variable. |
| throughput 2T, u64, write-heavy | `scc` | 693.885 µs | 60.299 µs | 8.7% | 6.0% | Noisy middle of the concurrent field; not a close contender. |

The practical rule: do not use the 1M growth results to separate implementations that are within ~10% of each other. The 1M String variants for `rpds-hash-trie-map`, `papaya`, and `imbl` have relMAD of 11–25%, and the `rustc-hash` vs `indexmap` gap at 1M String is 1%. The 100K String `imbl` result (20.6% relMAD) is similarly unsuitable for fine-grained ranking.

### Fast implementations with noisy tails

A low relMAD does not guarantee a quiet tail. These are fast or close contenders whose standard deviation is high relative to the median; the median is stable, but occasional slow samples can flip close rankings.

| Benchmark | Implementation | Median | MAD | relMAD | relSD | Why it matters |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---|
| growth 1M, out-of-box, u64 | `rustc-hash` | 19.810 ms | 277.370 µs | 1.4% | 19.9% | Fastest, but the tail is wide; a single run should not be used to declare a small win. |
| growth 1M, out-of-box, u64 | `hashbrown` | 21.950 ms | 176.104 µs | 0.8% | 20.0% | Close contender with a wide tail; medians are more trustworthy than means. |
| contention 3T, u64, uniform | `leapfrog` | 249.671 µs | 2.318 µs | 0.9% | 20.9% | Fastest, but high tail variance; the 1.13× lead over `txmap` is not rock-solid. |
| contention 3T, u64, Zipf 1.0 | `txmap` | 316.955 µs | 4.004 µs | 1.3% | 20.9% | Runner-up to `leapfrog`; close rankings under skew should be repeated. |
| sync 3T, String<32>, read-only | `papaya` | 324.549 µs | 6.233 µs | 1.9% | 18.2% | Fastest and 2.30× ahead; the lead is large enough to survive the noise. |
| throughput 3T, u64, read-heavy | `leapfrog` | 205.103 µs | 5.447 µs | 2.7% | 15.7% | Fastest; the 1.29× lead over `txmap` is larger than the spread, but still repeat it. |
| lookup-hit, same-hasher, String<32> | `ahash` | 0.5722 µs | 0.0107 µs | 1.9% | 14.4% | Within 4% of `hashbrown`; the ordering of the top four is noise-sensitive. |

For context, the fast-benchmark MADs in this table are sub-microsecond to sub-millisecond, while the 1M growth MADs are milliseconds to tens of milliseconds. For the fast cases, high `relSD` is driven by rare slow samples rather than a broad distribution; for 1M growth, the spread is genuinely broad. That is exactly why the report uses medians: the median remains stable while the mean and standard deviation are inflated by tail events. The correct operational response is to repeat the specific benchmark on the target hardware before making a close call, not to switch implementations based on a 1–5% difference.

### Stability summary

- **Trust the large gaps:** `indexmap` iteration (2.0–2.6×), `intmap` u64 point operations (1.34–1.45×), `rustc-hash` serial throughput (1.17–1.52×), `leapfrog` 3T u64 Zipf 2.0 contention (3.10×), and `papaya` 3T String read-only synchronization (2.30×) are well outside the noise.
- **Treat small gaps as ties:** 3T String write-heavy `scc` vs `txmap` (1%), 1M String growth `rustc-hash` vs `indexmap` (1%), and the same-hasher u64 lookup cluster (2–10%) should not be used to choose an implementation without repeated measurement.
- **Be cautious with 1M growth and `create`:** both have high relative spread and are sensitive to allocator/cache effects. Use them for order-of-magnitude guidance, not fine ranking.
- **Some fast results are artifacts or structural effects:** sub-nanosecond `clone` for `rpds-hash-trie-map`, ~9–10 ns for `imbl`/`immutable-chunkmap`, and ~90 ns for `starshard` are O(1)/shallow clones, not deep copies. They are stable but not comparable to deep-clone costs.

## 5. Implementation Limitations

This section reflects the constraints visible in `src/maps/` and the benchmark harness. It is not a full API review of each crate; it is the subset that affects these results. All lookups use `get_cloned`, so they include cloning `V`; with `V = u64` that cost is negligible, but with large values it would be part of the measured lookup time. All inserts/removes are also wrapper calls, not necessarily the crate's most ergonomic native API.

### Per-implementation notes

- **`ahash`** — `AHashMap<K, V, H>`; point ops require `K: Hash + Eq`, `V: Clone`; `H: BuildHasher` (plus `Default` for `new`); map clone additionally requires `K: Clone`, `V: Clone`, `H: Clone`. No missing operations. Clone is deep (38.0 µs at 100K). The default hasher is ahash, so the same-hasher groups are effectively its native configuration.
- **`btreemap`** — `BTreeMap`; `K: Ord` (not `Hash`), `K: Clone` for clone, `V: Clone`; no custom hasher. Mutation requires `&mut`; no `BenchMapInsert`/`GetOrInsert` through `&self`, so it cannot enter concurrent groups. Ordered iteration and range queries are its differentiator, but ranges are not benchmarked here.
- **`crossbeam-skiplist`** — `SkipMap`; `K: Ord`; mutation requires `K: Ord + Send + 'static`, `V: Send + 'static`; `get_or_insert` requires `K/V: Send + 'static`. No hasher; no `Clone` (bench comments say it does not implement Clone), so it is absent from clone and clone-then-write. Concurrent `&self` mutation is supported; reads use epoch-based reclamation.
- **`dashmap`** — `DashMap<K, V, H>`; `K: Hash + Eq`, `H: BuildHasher + Clone + Default` for `new`; `V: Clone` for owned get/remove; map clone requires `K: Clone`, `V: Clone`, `H: Clone`. Sharded `RwLock`; concurrent `&self` mutation. Clone is deep (67.0 µs at 100K).
- **`hashbrown`** — `HashMap<K, V, H>`; `K: Hash + Eq` for point ops, `H: BuildHasher`; map clone requires `K: Clone`, `V: Clone`, `H: Clone`. No interior mutability. The out-of-the-box default hasher is the crate's default (foldhash), which differs from `std`'s `RandomState`; same-hasher groups remove that difference.
- **`hashlink`** — `LinkedHashMap<K, V, H>`; `H: Default` for `new`, `H: BuildHasher` for ops, `K: Hash + Eq`; clone requires `K: Clone`, `V: Clone`, `H: Clone`. No `&self` mutation. Clone is deep and very slow (42.0 µs at 1K; 3.76 ms at 100K). Linked-list ordering is the feature; it is not exercised by these workloads.
- **`horde`** — `SyncTable`; insert requires `K: Clone + Hash + Eq`, `V: Clone`; get/remove require `K: Hash + Eq`, `V: Clone`; clear requires `K: Hash`; clone requires `K: Clone + Hash`, `V: Clone`, `H: Clone`. No `get_or_insert` in the wrapper; writes go through `&mut`, so it is absent from concurrent throughput/synchronization despite epoch-based reads. Read paths use `horde::collect::pin`. Clone is 923.9 µs at 100K.
- **`imbl`** — `GenericHashMap<K, V, H, DefaultSharedPtr>` (persistent HAMT); `H: Default` for `new`; mutation (`insert`, `get_or_insert`, `remove`) requires `K: Clone + Hash + Eq`, `V: Clone`; clone requires `K/V: Clone`, `H: Clone`. No `&self` mutation, so serial only. Structural sharing makes clone ~10 ns, but path-copying makes writes expensive: clone-then-write is 13.0/86.9/1295.9 µs at 1K/10K/100K.
- **`immutable-chunkmap`** — `MapM<K, V>`; `K: Clone + Ord`, `V: Clone` for essentially every operation; no hasher. No `clear`; no `&self` mutation; clone is O(1)/structural (~9 ns). COW operations are `insert_cow`, `remove_cow`, and `get_or_insert_cow`. Ordered by key, unlike the hash-trie maps.
- **`indexmap`** — `IndexMap<K, V, H>`; `H: BuildHasher + Default`, `K: Hash + Eq`; clone requires `K: Clone`, `V: Clone`, `H: Clone`. No interior mutability. Preserves insertion order; iteration wins, but point operations, insertion, and removal carry index bookkeeping and are slower. `clear` retains capacity.
- **`intmap`** — `IntMap<K, V>`; `K: IntKey` (integer keys only), `V: Clone` for clone/get/remove; mutation requires `&mut`; no hasher. `IntKey` is the binding constraint: no `String`, no same-hasher groups, no concurrent use. Clone is deep (24.8 µs at 1K; 2.94 ms at 100K). No hashing means no HashDoS surface, but the key type is severely restricted.
- **`leapfrog`** — `LeapMap<K, V, H>`; `K: Eq + Hash + Copy`, `V: Value` (Copy values), `H: BuildHasher + Default`. `Copy` keys and values are hard restrictions: no `String` keys, no non-`Copy` payloads. No `Clone` (bench comments say it does not implement Clone) and no `clear`; `&self` insert/remove/get_or_insert are available. `new` uses `with_capacity_and_hasher(0, ...)`; Cargo enables `stable_alloc`. Strong concurrent u64 results, narrow applicability.
- **`papaya`** — `HashMap<K, V, H>`; `H: BuildHasher + Default`, `K: Hash + Eq`; `V: Clone` for get/remove/get_or_insert; `&self` mutation. Clone requires `K: Clone + Hash + Eq`, `V: Clone`, `H: Clone`, and is deep (5.66 ms at 100K). Lock-free reads; best String read-only synchronization, not a write-heavy generalist.
- **`rpds-hash-trie-map`** — `HashTrieMap<K, V, archery::RcK, H>`; `K: Eq + Hash`, `H: BuildHasher + Clone` (plus `Default` for `new`), `V: Clone` for get/remove. No `clear`, no `get_or_insert`, no `&self` mutation. Uses `RcK`, i.e. `Rc`-style single-thread sharing, so it is not `Send`/`Sync` as configured. Clone is O(1) (~0.8 ns); mutation is path-copying.
- **`rustc-hash`** — `FxHashMap<K, V>` with fixed `FxBuildHasher`; `K: Hash + Eq`; clone requires `K: Clone`, `V: Clone`. No `new_with_hasher` in the wrapper and no interior mutability. No HashDoS protection: FxHash is deterministic and non-cryptographic. Fastest serial map for trusted keys, but the security trade-off is real.
- **`scc`** — `HashMap<K, V, H>`; `H: BuildHasher + Default`, `K: Hash + Eq`; `V: Clone` for get/remove/get_or_insert; `&self` mutation. Clone requires `K: Clone + Hash + Eq`, `V: Clone`, `H: Clone`, and is deep (1.79 ms at 100K). Sharded concurrent hash map; only a marginal 3T String write-heavy win.
- **`starshard`** — `ShardedHashMap<K, V, H>`; `K: Clone + Hash + Eq + Send + Sync`, `V: Clone + Send + Sync`, `H: BuildHasher + Clone + Send + Sync` (plus `Default` for `new`); `DEFAULT_SHARDS` shards; `&self` mutation. Clone is ~90 ns at all sizes, consistent with structural/shallow sharing rather than a deep copy. It has no win or close result in these workloads.
- **`std`** — `std::collections::HashMap<K, V, H>`; `H: BuildHasher + Default`, `K: Hash + Eq`; `V: Clone` for get/remove; clone requires `K: Clone`, `V: Clone`, `H: Clone`. No interior mutability. The default `RandomState` is the DoS-resistant baseline; same-hasher results show its table structure is close to `hashbrown`/`ahash`.
- **`txmap`** — `TxMap<K, V, MutexPolicy, H>` via `TxMapBuilder`; `H: BuildHasher + Default`, `K: Hash + Eq`; `get_or_insert` requires `K: Clone`; `V: Clone` for owned get/remove; `&self` mutation. Clone requires `K: Clone`, `V: Clone`, `H: Clone` and takes 79.6 µs at 100K. Best general concurrent map in this suite.

### Cross-cutting restrictions

- **No memory or tail-latency data.** The suite measures wall-clock medians, not bytes per entry, allocator fragmentation, or p99/p999 latency. A map that wins the median can still have a worse tail.
- **Value clones are included in reads.** `get_cloned` and owned `remove` clone `V`. With `V = u64`, this is negligible; with a large value type, lookup and removal rankings could change.
- **Key types are limited.** Most workloads use `u64` or `String<32>`; key sensitivity adds `String<16>` and `String<128>`. There are no composite keys, custom `Hash` implementations, or adversarial collision sets. `rustc-hash`'s lack of DoS resistance is therefore not stress-tested here.
- **Concurrent workloads use a fixed worker pool.** Threads are spawned and pinned once per sample, outside the timed region, so thread-creation costs are excluded. The timed region is the workload plus the pool's release/join synchronization. Results are specific to the machine and thread counts tested (2 and 3).
- **Persistent-map clone semantics differ.** `clone` for `rpds-hash-trie-map`, `imbl`, `immutable-chunkmap`, and (apparently) `starshard` is structural, not a deep copy. Comparing those times to deep-cloning maps is not apples-to-apples; `clone-then-write` is the fairer test of copy-on-write behavior.
- **`clear-and-reuse` and `growth` use flat sampling.** They are the only families configured with `SLOW_SAMPLING_MODE = Flat`, so their sample schedules differ from the linear-sampling families. This is appropriate for slow benchmarks but is worth remembering when comparing variance across families.
- **No `concread`, `concurrent_map`, or `flurry`.** `concread` and `flurry` were too slow; `concurrent_map` is `Send` but not `Sync`, so the harness would need a thread-local workaround that changes what is measured. They are not part of the 19-implementation comparison.

## 6. Recommendations

### Further investigation

| Area | Recommended follow-up | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Long and adversarial keys | Extend `String<64>`/`String<128>` coverage to lookup, growth, and throughput; add composite keys, custom `Hash` implementations, and collision-heavy key sets. | At `String<128>`, only `ahash` and `hashbrown` are within 10% of the winner, and lookup cost is ~2.9× the `String<16>` cost. The suite has no adversarial collision test, so `rustc-hash`'s lack of HashDoS resistance is not quantified. |
| Serial String write-heavy | Repeat the `imbl` result across runs and machines, and profile its insert path. | This is the only serial group where `rustc-hash` is not clearly ahead: `imbl` is within 9% (824.059 µs vs 759.384 µs). It is a narrow exception, but worth understanding before dismissing persistent maps for write-heavy work. |
| 3-thread String write-heavy | Repeat `scc` vs `txmap` with more samples, 4+ threads, and larger operation counts. | `scc` is only 1% faster (1.093 ms vs 1.102 ms), which is inside the observed noise. Do not choose `scc` for this result alone. |
| Concurrent u64 skew | Re-run `leapfrog` contention/synchronization with more thread counts and key distributions; inspect allocator and reclamation behavior. | `leapfrog`'s 3.10× Zipf 2.0 contention win is large, but its 3T uniform contention case has relSD 20.9%; the size and stability of the skew advantage need confirmation. |
| Snapshot/copy-on-write workloads | Benchmark clone-then-read, clone-then-occasional-write, and retained-snapshot patterns, not only clone-then-write-immediately. | Structural clones (`rpds-hash-trie-map`, `imbl`, `immutable-chunkmap`) look artificially good in the clone-only table and artificially bad when every clone is immediately mutated. Real snapshot workloads sit between those extremes. |
| Concurrent clone/replacement | Measure `txmap` clone and whole-map replacement under concurrency. | `txmap` is the best general concurrent map here, but its clone takes 79.6 µs at 100K; clone-heavy or map-swap workloads could change the recommendation. |
| Ordered-map operations | Add range scans, ordered iteration, and predecessor/successor queries for `btreemap` and `immutable-chunkmap`. | These capabilities are their main differentiators, and the current suite does not measure them. |
| Get-or-insert hit ratio | Test 90/10 and other cache-style hit ratios instead of only the current 50/50 mix. | The repository defines `GET_OR_INSERT_HIT_RATIO = 0.90`, but the benchmark uses 10K existing plus 10K missing keys. Different hit ratios change the read/write balance and could reorder the contenders. |
| Memory and tail latency | Add bytes per entry, allocation counts, and p99/p999 latency measurements. | The suite measures wall-clock medians only. A map that wins the median can still have unacceptable memory use or tail latency. |

### Measurement quality issues

- **1M growth is not precise enough for fine ranking.** The 1M String variants for `rpds-hash-trie-map`, `papaya`, and `imbl` have relMAD of 11–25%, and the 1% `rustc-hash` vs `indexmap` gap (98.970 ms vs 99.583 ms) is inside that noise. Treat 1M results as order-of-magnitude guidance.
- **`create` is allocator-sensitive.** It times 10,000 empty-map constructions as one batch, and `indexmap` has 13.0% relMAD. The absolute differences are small; use `create` to identify gross setup costs, not to rank maps within a few percent.
- **The clone table is not a deep-copy comparison.** Sub-nanosecond to ~90 ns clone times for persistent/sharded maps are structural or shallow operations. Use `clone-then-write` for copy-on-write decisions and benchmark the actual snapshot lifetime.
- **Fast benchmarks can still have noisy tails.** Several leading results have relSD around 15–21% even when relMAD is low (for example, 1M growth, 3T contention, and 3T synchronization). Repeat close calls on the target hardware.
- **Out-of-the-box and same-hasher results answer different questions.** Out-of-the-box combines map design with hasher choice; same-hasher isolates map structure under ahash. Choose the hasher policy first, then use the appropriate table.
- **Concurrent results are machine- and thread-count-specific.** Workers are pinned and reused outside the timed region, and only 2 and 3 threads are tested. Validate on the deployment topology and thread counts you actually use.
- **No memory or production-tail data.** Wall-clock medians and run-to-run MAD/SD say nothing about bytes per entry, fragmentation, or p99/p999 latency. Add those measurements before making capacity-planning decisions.
